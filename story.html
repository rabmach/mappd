HOW YOU LIVIN' — state of USA now with updates and additions


There were 39 million of us early.  Early voting this year beat 2022's whole arc, and that's the quiet headline: people who usually sit out decided to sit in.  Nov 3 is the vote; the record is already written around it.

The government is funded until Dec 11.  That's not stability, that's a soft mattress before the real rumble — the actual showdown is the lame-duck session after the election, when somebody has to pay for the calendar the last year drew.  The ledger so far, though, so nobody forgets:

  • The longest full shutdown ever recorded — 43 days, Oct–Nov 2025, and the ACA subsidy fuse it lit,
  • A brief January lapse, 3 days nobody noticed,
  • A 76-day shutdown of one department alone — DHS — where the country stopped funding the agency but

Quietly left enforcement running, which tells you exactly which parts of government they consider load-bearing.

A billion dollars in rescissions got clipped out of the budget mid-year.  That's the theme of the era, stated plainly: things get taken away not by debate and vote, but by memo.

The money late-2026 hits hardest, by design. The big domestic law passed in July 2025 spread ~$1.3 trillion of cuts on a timeline that lands after the midterms.  That's not conspiracy, it's calendar: SNAP's new work requirements bite Dec 31, 2026; Medicaid's punch lands late 2026 into 2027.  Meanwhile food assistance has already shrunk by millions of people and — newer this fall — the federal government started asking states to co-pay for the poor.  New Mexico is the only state that answered with its own money to soften ACA premiums: net monthly cost for a middle-of-market plan jumped from $113 to $178, deductibles up a thousand plus, enrollment sliding toward 17% down.

2027 open enrollment starts Nov 1 — the day after the vote. The map held the case out West. Some 8.1 billion dollars' worth of fewer visits forgone to workplaces and shops scrambling around federal enforcement wages, plus $14 billion gone from local economies in year one.  Interior arrests rose from 28% to 39% of all enforcement.  Fresno lost harvest-season labor.  West Virginia's Eastern Panhandle saw framing costs triple when the installers stopped coming.  Beef country — Kansas, Oklahoma, Texas — pushed back out loud, so we know exactly whose business model bends when the floor crew runs.  Neighbors call that inflation.  It's also exactly "how you livin'," point for point.

The guard stories narrowed but didn't end. Federal guards pulled back from Los Angeles, Chicago and Portland — court rules on domestic use held, and the Supreme Court rebuffed the relief in Chicago — but one full year plus in Washington, D.C.  the deployment never left, which tells you how the rule gets decided: by argument, city by city, for as long as anyone draws breath to argue it.

The corruption ledger, because mappd keeps one. Two hundred twelve enforcement actions canceled against 205 corporations.  $210.5 million flowed into one party's PACs while the actions sat closing-stamped — $100M of it from one man's pledge, more from crypto interests.  Seventy-plus pardons, mostly fraud, erasing about $119 million owed to victims.  The cash-on-video official had his case quietly zeroed out.  The federal public-integrity unit went from 40 lawyers on ~195 matters to roughly 20, taking the ¯\_(ツ)_/¯ with them.  And a senator on camera next to stacked hundred dollar bricks is just — this spring's costume.  That's not a paragraph's worth of anger.  That's the audit speaker notes.

Redistricting is real war economics. Thirty-four point eight million Americans — one in ten — now live in a district redrawn this cycle to shift power before they drive a single block to vote. Texas drew, Democrats walked, and polling a month out has the fragile new map at risk in 3 of 5 Southern-Texas races.  One senator's health scare opened a whole Senate seat nobody was promised — Kentucky's race now measures one more vote on whether a future Congress can break its own rules by rumor.  Makes you proud of how much arithmetic happens at one polling place.

The therapies that landed, so the ledger isn't only losses. mappd's about what's on the map, but here the map is medicine:

  • Twice-yearly HIV prevention. One shot every six months, FDA-cleared June 2025.  In trials, ~4,300 people, 2 infections total — that number has no rhetorical equal.  Generic licenses went royalty-free across most of the planet, and State Dept is up to millions of doses abroad.  Here's the piece that matters for the US: insurer pickup of the drug happened in months, seeking circles-of-loyalty money — proof that when a big money system actually wants a drug to flow, it flows very fast.  Cultural conquest: high five.
  • The pill for weight, at $25. FDA-cleared April 2026.  First non-injectable, non-molecule, no-food-restriction one.  $25/month with insurance; $149 raw; Part D took it at $50 starting July. Half of employer plans won't cover existing obesity meds — that's the collision to circle: drug that exists, price that works, coverage that chooses to not.  Track Attribute: that wall is a policy, not a fact of the drug.
  • mRNA cancer vaccine — first ever to win a phase 3. Alongside standard melanoma therapy, the personalized shot met its endpoints this August.  Not "improves odds." The vaccine class proved itself.  This is the one your grandkids will check blood pressure over.
  • The CRISPR cure's barbell. First gene-edited cure is real.  It costs $2.2M and the whole 2025 count of Americans who received it is 64 — while ~100,000 Americans carry the disease.  A CMS experiment now lets Medicaid pay by outcome instead of sticker, which is honest finance catching up to honest medicine, and barely got press.
  • And the promises walled shut, because you're entitled to both halves. 383 clinical trials were terminated outright — 74,000 enrolled human beings left holding nothing.  Over 7,500 research grants suspended or cancelled; court orders reinstated what they could but something like $1.4 billion remains blocked still.  An already-funded, already-running HIV vaccine center lost $129M in funding mid-stream — stopped Recruiting.  All formula, no essay: this was the choice.

The builds are the balance entry, because mappd refuses all-crisis. This is the "how we save loot" chapter, and it's not a wishlist, it's happening:

  • Colorado ran the biggest state-level bet on factory-built homes anywhere: eight factories at one shot, one maker recycling shipping containers into homes *30% cheaper than traditional construction*, a production ceiling of ~6,500 homes a year if the trade reaches it.
  • California put $10M into scaling factory-built houses, capped local inspection and permit fees for those homes at 50% of the site-built rate, and got out of the drills.
  • A Los Angeles factory closed a $32M working-capital facility (Oct 2026) built specifically to fix the reason modular housing kept dying at the bank: lenders refusing to fund a house that exists in a factory but isn't screwed into land yet.  800 units on deck, 2,500 in pipeline.
  • Austin already taught this map 3D printing; the purge continues — 245+ printed structures done, and new tool ownership programs point at wall systems around $20 a square foot, roughly 40% under domestic framing cost.  Montana quietly raised the first big modular mass-timber building — 95 homes of workforce housing in 11 months, on time and under budget, which is practically a folklore event in construction.

The bill on your meter, wear it well — data centers. This is the largest physical construction boom in America and it's paid for in a place nobody budgets for: your electric bill.

  • One grid market — PJM, the largest in the country, 13 states — ran its annual capacity auction and the price went up 833% in one step ($28.92 to $269.92/MW-day).  The independent monitor pinned the cause: data centers drove 63% of that jump — $9.3 billion spread across homeowners of the region whether they've ever opened a chatbot or not.  June 2025: New Jersey residential bills jumped 17–20%.  First quarter of this year: wholesale power across the same grid **+76% year-over-year**, and the monitor's own words are the ouch: the price impacts are "very large and not reversible."
  • The structural mismatch is the story you can tell anybody: **a power plant takes 6–7 years to build, a data center takes 2–3.** Fifteen gigawatts of plants have retired since 2022; 70 gigawatts of new large load is queued by 2038.  The grid was built for a country that wasn't about to double its appetite.
  • But here's the entry mappd exists for: **the same boom produced the opposite outcomes, by design choice. Portland General Electric, June 2026: data centers pay +29%** tariffs; residential bills went -1.3%.  Same grid, same quarter, one set of regulatory choices.  A PG&E executive goes further — every gigawatt of new load can cut your bill a percent, "if you get the pricing right." So it's not whether data centers raise your bill.  It's whether your state made them put their money where their megawatts are.
  • Washington and the states are arguing in real time: a Ratepayer Protection Pledge signed by seven of the biggest AI companies — voluntary, non-binding (it's a pledge; it protects at the speed of a promise).  A Senate bill would have regulators merely consider protections for facilities over 100 MW.  Pennsylvania went at it by executive order in September with hard guardrails (GRID requirements + price caps, claiming $30B+ spared for consumers).  And federal officials have openly floated breaking up PJM itself — the biggest grid operator in the land, now under breakup threat because bill shock became a midterm issue.
  • The counterweight is local, same as it ever was: data centers employ almost nobody once built — a huge load, not a factory town — but they need land, water, and interconnection approval, and so for the first time in years county zoning boards and farm towns have leverage.  Wisconsin, Michigan, rural Pennsylvania are rejecting or extracting (PA farmer Bobbi Thompson's water worry is the standing portrait).  Closed-loop cooling cuts water use up to 10x but burns more electricity — every trade in this story is at someone's kitchen table.

The pond doesn't stop at the shoreline. The rest of the world always gets a vote, and lately >it's voting with its procurement budget.  June 3, 2026: the European Commission adopted a whole Technological Sovereignty Package — Chips Act 2.0, a Cloud and AI Development Act, a first-of-its-kind open-source strategy — full industrial policy, adopted in one sitting.  The plane-English read: Europe's own cloud companies fell from 29% market share in 2017 to 15% by 2022, the continent allowed itself to become what their own documents now call a "digital colony," and the acuteness in the language ("suppliers responding to non-European rules") is calibrated directly to what's happened in Washington.  Eighteen months ago "EuroStack" was a pitch document; now it's in coalition plans from Berlin to Paris, and German healthcare providers are choosing sovereign cloud because US privacy rules "don't apply" to their patients.  None of this is sentiment.  It's procurement.  And it lands here too: when your largest customers start building their own everything, the services economy that fed American towns for decades starts thinning from the far edge inward.  mappd watches it because it's "how you livin'" with an ocean in the way.

Wars, always on, same chapter: somebody's town absorbs it, somebody's rates pay for it, somebody's dockworker counts it — the map never needed the border drawn at the waterline to feel it.

Farmland is anybody's kitchen table.  West Virginia signed its own side of this fork in real time, and it's worth its own stanza: a coal state announced roughly $96 billion in new private investment in under a year — one day alone (Sept 2026) carried $81 billion of it: NScale's $69.2B phase eating 2 gigawatts of power with 645 permanent jobs at the end, Starwood's $12B in Mason County at 2.16 GW, Penzance's $4B "High Impact Intelligence Center" in the Eastern Panhandle — the same counties where federal enforcement stops had already tripled local framing costs.  The governor's pitch: "If you need energy, come to West Virginia.  If you need workers, come to West Virginia." The arithmetic underneath: the state treasurer's own analysis puts the two Mason County projects' tax yield at ~$231M a year against ~$77B invested — and the deal everyone's watching is that both companies pledged to self-fund their grid infrastructure instead of dropping it on ratepayers.  The American chestnut orchard in Mason County — the one they've spent a century trying to restore — is the standing ecology-on-the-docket item.  And the Public Service Commission is writing West Virginia's data-center electricity rules right now, with the Sierra Club and the Coal Association commenting in the same docket.  Whatever shape that rule takes, it goes in the ledger: small states are not waiting for Congress on this one.

The lone-persons-worth-remembering chapter. Burlington, Vermont's land trust model now guards 2,800+ households across 40 years and still doesn't blow up affordability on resale.  Houston's Housing First coalition went from 8,500 unsheltered to 3,200 in about a decade, cheapest of any major city, by refusing the shelter-first philosophy.  Cooperation Jackson, in the state that leads every bad-water and bad-lit chart, builds an entire mini-economy — printing, farms, lawn crews — owned by the people who live there.  And the SNAPSHOT-file story: one civic organizer turned a church budget into a national AIDS walk — $224,000 worth of sponsors said yes.  None of those reached their promised glory.  All three did the work they were asked to do, in line with the micro-brave practice: show up alone, get a yes, hold the door for no one, and let the record keep score instead of applause.  The gesture is the artifact.  The gesture is survivorship.
